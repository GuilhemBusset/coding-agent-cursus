Talk track: Act I ended on one forward pass per token, the same work however hard the question: a glance. Point at the box “One forward pass”: its seven squares are Act I’s whole graph. Around it, this act: the tokens it writes and reads back, its answer, and a check outside the model.

The badges are the act’s five questions; point at each. One, the loop: how does it think? Two, the check: is it right? Three, the dial: how much thinking? Four, the written tokens: trust what it wrote? Five, the context: what does it know?

The puzzle: eight parcels, each with a weight and a value, and a van for 63 kg. Each goes whole or stays behind. Which load is worth the most?

Activity: a glance first, no writing; then a pencil. Who was sure at a glance? Who reached for a pencil? Press the parcels the room calls out; past 63 kg the load spills out in red. Do not say which load is best: part 3 runs the check.

Transition: “You reached for a pencil. A model does the same: it writes. That is question one.”

Background for you, not for the slides: Act I’s pass is the same 12 layers in GPT-2 small. Most start from B and D, light and valuable. Pressing a parcel loads or unloads it; the readout only adds kg and EUR and never says which load is best. Part 1 shows a model’s answer before part 3’s check settles it.

Part 1 · How does it think?

Thinking is writing: each token buys another pass.

Speaker notes

Talk track: below the graph, badge 1’s loop, unrolled. Point at the row of passes: each is Act I’s whole graph, a fixed stack of layers. A pass hands its work on only by writing a token: follow an arrow down to the tape and up into the next pass.

Point at the amber bracket: “System 2”, Kahneman’s label for slow, deliberate thinking, is here only a metaphor for this loop: the same weights writing more tokens, not a second module. Never say it “switches to System 2”.

Now the tape, a real reply from Claude Opus 5.5 at low effort. The hatched box: 286 thinking tokens, billed but returned empty; any working is in there, unread. Then the answer, stated first: 229 EUR. Then the steps, each reusing a number above: B and D leave 44 kg; E and G fill 43; 116 plus 113 make 229. Hidden or shown, this writing is the chain of thought, or trace.

For your agent: for multi-step work, ask for the steps first; length buys steps, not correctness.

Transition: “It says 229 is the best. Is it right, and how would we know?”

Background for you, not for the slides: the stack is 12 layers in GPT-2 small, unpublished for frontier models. Within a pass, a token reads earlier tokens only at its own layer, so only a written token carries the top of one pass to the bottom of the next: Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023, detailed in the instructor notes. The thinking is billed as output, and Claude Code returns it empty. Kahneman’s book is Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011. The full reply also lists A and G for 228 EUR, just below 229; the tape skips it, and the effort demo’s check shows it.

Part 2 · Is it right? · Lab

Many tries pay off only when a check can pick.

Source: Bradley Brown et al., Large Language Monkeys, arXiv, 2024.

Speaker notes

Talk track: a program can check the van by trying all 256 loads; part 3 runs it. The model samples: ask N times, keep the best. Point at the two lines: 100 tries, then 10,000. When the answer key picks, problems solved climb from 82.9% to 98.4%. When a vote or a scoring model picks, the best of three methods goes from 40.5% to 41.4%. Point at the red band: the right answer is in the pile, and nothing picks it. A vote keeps the commonest answer, even a wrong one.

Predict question, in the Best-of-N panel, where the check is a “verifier” and the pile “coverage”: if the verifier passes one wrong answer in ten, which curve reaches 100% as tries grow without end? Everyone locks in first.

Activity: move N from 1 to 1,024, then raise the false-positive rate: the verifier’s pick falls below coverage. Reveal; illustrative numbers. The close deck’s exit ticket asks exactly this.

For your agent: sampling is cheap, selecting is not: decide what picks the winner: tests, a checker or you.

Transition: “A check can pick an answer. It can also train the model.”

Background for you, not for the slides: Brown et al., Large Language Monkeys: MATH, Llama-3-8B-Instruct; exact values 82.9% to 98.44%, and 40.50% to 41.41%. The scoring model is a reward model, a second model that scores answers. The grey line is whichever of three vote and reward-model methods gained most; in the paper’s Fig. 7 that is the majority vote, and the reward models gained less or fell. Its plateau sits near 100 tries in the paper’s figures, “beyond several hundred samples” in its abstract. The panel’s numbers are illustrative, the shape the paper’s; its own wording: “With a 10% false-positive verifier, as N grows without bound, which curve’s limit is 100%?” Advice: before asking for five attempts, decide what picks.

Part 2 · Is it right? · Training

A check can also train a model to reason.

Source: DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-R1 incentivizes reasoning in LLMs through reinforcement learning, Nature, 2025.

Speaker notes

Predict question: “A rule marks only the final answer, right or wrong. Do the answers get shorter or longer?”

Talk track: in the graph, the check now trains the weights. Walk the ring: reinforcement learning, RL. DeepSeek-R1-Zero starts from a base model, with no supervised fine-tuning. It writes thinking, then an answer; a rule checks the final answer and format; a right answer earns a reward; the weights move toward it; next question. On AIME 2024, a hard maths contest, the first-try score rose from 15.6% to 77.9%, and the answers grew longer, with checking, reflection and second approaches. Act I named this: RL with verifiable rewards.

Qualification to keep: score and length both jumped when the length limit doubled; and this is R1-Zero, the pure-RL experiment, not the multistage DeepSeek-R1.

Point at the amber chip: does RL sharpen what the base could already do, or expand it? Still open: pick no winner.

For your agent: give it a check: a test, an oracle, a solver.

Transition: “So: only a check can say. Next: how much should it think? A dial you set.”

Background for you, not for the slides: the base model is DeepSeek-V3; the rule compares the final answer with ground truth. The first-try score is the average pass@1 (Fig. 1a); with self-consistency decoding it reaches 86.7%. From the Methods: score and length both jump at step 8.2k, where the length limit went from 32,768 to 65,536 tokens. The term is Lambert et al.’s (Tülu 3). Sharpen: Yue et al.; expand: ProRL, Liu et al.; different models, tasks and training lengths; open as of 2026-10-18. The amber chip links to a lab panel whose curves are invented. These models were trained where answers can be checked.

Part 3 · How much thinking? · Lab

More thinking can cost accuracy, not just money.

Source: Aryo Pradipta Gema et al., Inverse Scaling in Test-Time Compute, arXiv, 2025.

Speaker notes

Predict question, asked as you advanced. Read it in full: “You have an apple and an orange … 61% probability that they are exactly a Red Delicious apple and a Navel orange. Calculate how many fruits you have.” Always 2. Shortest reply or longest: which is right more often?

Talk track: here more thinking moves accuracy the wrong way. Point at the stack marked ×5: five such distractors per question, in tasks built to find this. Point at the arrow: DeepSeek R1, five replies per question ranked by length, is right 70% of the time with its shortest, 30% with its longest. Longer reasoning gets pulled into the probability. The effect varies by model.

Qualification to keep: built to find the effect: a warning, not an average.

Activity, optional: the effort-dial panel, inverse-scaling task on; its illustrative curve bends down past its peak.

For your agent: if a high-effort run wanders, rerun lower, cut irrelevant context, judge by the check, not the length.

Transition, carrying the next predict: “If the thinking can wander, can we at least read it? Guess first: when a hint changes the answer, how often does the reasoning mention it?”

Background for you, not for the slides: Gema et al., Inverse Scaling in Test-Time Compute. The fruit question is their Fig. 1; the arrow is Misleading Math, Fig. 3. Not on the slide, because it pools both setups: Claude Opus 4 goes from nearly 100% to around 85–90% as reasoning extends. In appendix I.1 of arXiv v2, on a similar question, how many games you have, Claude Opus 4 answers 2 with no reasoning, 26 with a 16,384-token budget.

Part 4 · Trust what it wrote?

Most traces omit the hint that changed the answer.

Source: Anthropic, Reasoning models don’t always say what they think, anthropic.com, 2025.

Speaker notes

Predict question, asked as you advanced: walk the strip first, take guesses, then press Reveal.

Talk track: a trace is faithful if it reflects what actually drove the answer. Walk the strip: a hint in the question, a professor’s opinion; the answer switches to it, so it mattered; does the trace say so? Point at the grids: per 100 answers the hint changed, Claude 3.7 Sonnet says so 25 times, DeepSeek R1 39; the empty cells stayed silent.

Qualification to keep, at the amber chip: multiple choice, contrived hints, tasks easy enough not to need the chain of thought. Conclude “check the evidence”, not “traces are useless”: a trace still tells you where to look.

In an agent, hints arrive through tool output: files, logs, web pages. Planted instructions measurably steer LLM applications; Act V returns to that. Hypothetical: a README calls the legacy/ tests flaky; the agent skips them, says “all relevant tests pass”, and never names the README.

For your agent: the reasoning is a lead; the evidence is the diff, the commands, the test run.

Transition: “Even with no hint at all, the explanation can be about a different process.”

Background for you, not for the slides: Anthropic’s study also planted fake metadata and an “unauthorized access” note; the 25 and 39 average over hint types. Test results are tool output too, and the tool-call transcript is better evidence than the reasoning. Planted instructions steering LLM applications is indirect prompt injection, Greshake et al., Not what you’ve signed up for; how often such cues go unmentioned in a trace is not measured.

Part 4 · Trust what it wrote?

A right answer can come with a wrong account.

Source: Jack Lindsey et al., On the Biology of a Large Language Model, transformer-circuits.pub, 2025.

Speaker notes

Predict question: “How would you add 36 + 59 in your head?” Most say: carry the 1.

Talk track: point at the left panel, what it did inside Claude 3.5 Haiku, two paths at once. Top lane, rough size, low precision: fuzzy features, “near 36” and “near 60”, give “near 92”. Bottom lane, exact: 6 plus 9 ends in 5. Together: 95, right. Now press “Ask it how”: it says it added the ones, carried the 1, then added the tens. Point at the question mark, now “not equal”: the school method, which the traced computation did not use. A right answer, with an account of a different process.

Qualification to keep: one model, one prompt: a vivid case, not a rate.

Back to the van, at the written tokens: part 1’s reply stated 229, then justified it: an explanation, not necessarily the route.

For your agent: ask for something you can rerun: the test, the computation, the command.

Transition, carrying the next predict: “Last question: what does the model know? Guess first: a model saw only lists of legal Othello moves, never the rules or the board. Is a board inside it?”

Background for you, not for the slides: the tracing method is attribution graphs, which show which internal features drive an output: Lindsey et al., On the Biology of a Large Language Model, section “Addition”. The model said: “I added the ones (6+9=15), carried the 1, then added the tens (3+5+1=9), resulting in 95.” The authors call it a capability without “metacognitive” insight into it. On the van, the route, if there was one, was in the 286 hidden thinking tokens.

Part 5 · What does it know?

“World model” means three things. Keep them apart.

  1. 1 Inside the network

    A state the network keeps inside.

  2. 2 A simulator

    A model of what happens next, used to plan.

  3. 3 The agent’s picture

    Built from what it read and ran: yours to feed.

Source: Kenneth Li et al., Emergent World Representations, arXiv, 2022.

Speaker notes

Predict question, asked as you advanced: take a few answers first.

Talk track: for the van, the whole world was in the prompt. Usually a model reasons from what it learned, an agent from what it read. Three meanings of “world model”: point at the three pictures.

Meaning 1, Othello-GPT, trained only on lists of legal moves. A probe, a small classifier reading its insides, recovers the board; edit that hidden board and its moves change. So yes: a board inside, and used.

Meaning 2, a simulator: from a state and an action, predict the next state; chain the predictions into an imagined plan, the dotted path around the obstacle.

Meaning 3, the agent’s picture of your repo, yours to control: read, not read, ran. tools/ and setup/ are holes; two slides on, we feed it.

For your agent: when someone says “world model”, ask which meaning, and what evidence.

Transition, carrying the next predict: “Tracking state is one thing; having the law behind it is another. Guess first: a model trained on orbits predicts them very well. Has it learned Newton’s law?”

Background for you, not for the slides: Li et al., Emergent World Representations: Othello-GPT predicts the next move in games of legal moves, never seeing the rules or the board; probes read a nonlinear representation of the board out of its activations, and the intervention makes it more than a correlation. Meaning 2 is Yann LeCun’s Mode-2, named by analogy with Kahneman (A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence, §3.1). Video and robot simulators are meaning 2: makers’ claims, dated in the instructor notes, none inside your coding agent.

Part 5 · What does it know? · Meaning 1 · Lab

Kepler without Newton: good predictions, the wrong laws.

Source: Keyon Vafa et al., What Has a Foundation Model Found?, arXiv, 2025.

Speaker notes

Predict question, asked as you advanced: take a few answers.

Talk track: meaning 1 again. Kepler’s laws predicted the orbits; Newton’s explained them and carried over to new problems. Point at the orbits, a schematic: a transformer trained on orbits predicts them almost perfectly. Fine-tune it to predict forces on five galaxy samples and ask what law its predictions imply: point at the red lines, a different law each time. Point at the tally: Newton recovered from the model, 0 of 5; from the true state, 5 of 5. Fitting the data is not having the law behind it.

Activity: in the world-model lab’s Kepler panel, let the fit match the orbits, then add a mass with the slider: the prediction breaks while the true orbit changes.

Qualification to keep: the panel is a stylized simulation, not the paper’s data.

For your agent: watch for confident pattern-matching where your repo differs from the many it has seen.

Transition, carrying the next predict: “What keeps the agent’s picture of your repo right? Guess first: two runs, same wrong picture; one plans once, the other checks where it is after every step. Which reaches the goal?”

Background for you, not for the slides: Vafa et al., What Has a Foundation Model Found? The fit is R² > 0.9999; the law comes from symbolic regression on its force predictions; the five laws are their Table 1. The control runs the same procedure on a model given the true state and recovers Newton for all five. Their reading: task-specific heuristics that do not generalize. An agent can predict well and still be wrong where your repo differs, especially after something changed.

Part 5 · What does it know? · Meaning 3 · Lab

Your repo, as the agent pictures it: feed it observations.

Speaker notes

Predict question, asked as you advanced: hands for plan once, replan, both, or neither. The answer is drawn.

Talk track: meaning 3, the one you control. The agent’s picture of your repo comes from what it read: files, AGENTS.md, earlier output. Point at the dashed outer track: test output comes back as observation.

Point at the two panels: the same wrong picture in both. Left, plan once: the dashed line, its picture, ends in the goal; the solid line, what happens, ends in the obstacle. Right, observe and replan: each dot checks where it really is; same first mistake, seen and corrected, and it reaches the goal. The dots are test output; replanning is the agent loop.

Qualification to keep, out loud: illustrative. Observing corrects the course, not the picture; it rescues a moderate error, and with a much worse picture it can fail too.

Think-pair-share: “On your own task, what could check the agent’s answer, and how often does it see that check?” Listen for pairs with no check.

For your agent: make observations cheap and frequent: fast tests, clear errors, an AGENTS.md.

Close · Five habits

Writing plus a check does what one glance cannot.

Speaker notes

Talk track: every badge has opened into its habit. One pass is a glance; writing chains steps, and a check outside the model turns many tries, or a training run, into results. Point at the two arrows into the context: the model’s own tokens, and what the world answers back.

One habit per badge, in order:

  • One, the loop: it writes, so ask for the steps: a plan before it acts.
  • Two, the check: only a check can say, so give it a test, an oracle or a solver, and trust that, not its say-so.
  • Three, the dial: more can hurt, so set effort per task: low for lookups, high when hard and checkable.
  • Four, the written tokens: a lead, not evidence, so read the diff, not the story: the commands, the test run.
  • Five, the context: its picture of your repo is yours to feed: feed it observations, fast tests, clear errors, an AGENTS.md.

Both labs stay open after class: test-time compute, and world models.

Transition into Act III: “The planner was a toy. In Act III the agent runs the check itself, with real tools, every turn: reality answers back.”
