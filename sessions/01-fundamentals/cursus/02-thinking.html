Fundamentals · Act II

Thinking

One puzzle, five questions: how a model thinks, whether its answer is right, how much it should think, whether to trust what it wrote, and what it knows.

Act II · Thinking

One forward pass is a glance. This puzzle needs steps.

Try it

Find the best load at a glance, without writing. Then take a pencil.

Speaker notes

Goal: students feel that some answers need several steps, meet the puzzle the whole act runs on, and see the five questions the act answers.

Talk track: Act I ended on one forward pass per token: the same 12 layers in GPT-2 small, the same amount of work however hard the question. Call that a glance. Now the puzzle: eight parcels, each with a weight and a value, and a van that carries 63 kg. Which load is worth the most?

Activity: give them a moment to answer at a glance, with no writing. Then let them use a pencil. Ask: “Who was sure at a glance? Who wanted to write something down?” Most reach for sums: B and D are light and valuable, so start there, then fill what is left. Do not give the answer: part 1 shows a model’s answer, and part 3 runs the demo and the check that settles it.

The strip at the top is the act: five questions, one per part. Read them aloud. Each part ends with its answer, the strip fills in as you go, and the close lists all five.

Transition: “You reached for a pencil. A model does the same: it writes. That is the first question.”

Part 1 · How does it think?

Thinking is writing: more tokens before the answer.

Why it helps

One pass
A fixed stack of layers.
Writing
Each token feeds every later pass.
System 2
This loop, a metaphor.

Source: William Merrill, Ashish Sabharwal, The Expressive Power of Transformers with Chain of Thought, arXiv, 2023.

Speaker notes

Goal: a model thinks by writing tokens it then reads. Each written token buys another pass, so the steps chain beyond the fixed depth of one pass. That writing is the chain of thought.

Talk track: this is a real reply to the puzzle, from Claude Opus 5.5 at low effort. It first wrote 286 thinking tokens, billed as output but returned empty by Claude Code: that is where it worked the puzzle out. Then the visible answer: it states 229 first, then shows steps. Point at the highlighted numbers: “That leaves 44 kg”, and A + G is exactly those 44 kg; 116 and 113 make 229. Each step reuses numbers written above, the way they did with a pencil. Hidden or shown, this writing is a chain of thought, also called the trace. Part 4 comes back to the order: the answer came before the steps you can read.

Why it helps: one pass climbs a fixed stack of layers, 12 in GPT-2 small (frontier models do not publish their depth). Within a layer, a token reads the earlier tokens at that same layer; only a written token carries the top layer’s result back to the bottom of the next pass. So every written token adds another stack of steps.

Background, if asked: earlier work found simple problems (are two nodes of a graph connected?) that a transformer answering immediately cannot solve, under standard complexity assumptions. Merrill and Sabharwal ask how much intermediate tokens add: a logarithmic number of steps adds little; a linear number recognizes all regular languages, under a technical assumption; a polynomial number gives exactly the polynomial-time problems. Say “under assumptions”. It is a result about capacity, not accuracy: training decides whether the model finds the right chain.

Say it, as Act I promised, pointing at the last line of the box: “System 2” is Kahneman’s label for slow, deliberate thinking (Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011). Bengio put it on the deep-learning agenda (NeurIPS 2019 Posner lecture); LeCun names a Mode-1 and a Mode-2 “by analogy” with Kahneman (A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence, §3.1). For a language model, System 2 is this loop: the same weights writing more tokens, not a second module. Do not say the model “switches to System 2”.

Driving: for multi-step work, let the agent write a plan or intermediate steps before it acts. Length buys steps, not correctness.

Transition: “It says 229 is the best, with 228 just below. Is it right, and how would we know?”

Part 2 · Is it right? · Lab

Many tries pay off only when a check can pick.

Lab

Open the Best-of-N panel

Predict, then move N and the check’s false-positive rate.

Source: Bradley Brown et al., Large Language Monkeys, arXiv, 2024.

Speaker notes

Goal: more tries help only when something outside the model can recognise a right one. A majority vote cannot; an answer key, a test or a solver can. The session’s exit ticket, in the close deck, asks exactly this.

Talk track: the model can answer differently each time (Act I: it samples). So ask it N times and keep the best. Best by what? Brown et al., on MATH with Llama-3-8B-Instruct: when the answer key picks, the share solved rises from 82.9% at 100 tries to 98.44% at 10,000. When a majority vote or a reward model (a second model trained to score answers) picks, the better of the two only moves from 40.50% to 41.41%. A vote keeps the most common answer, even a common wrong one. The paper puts the plateau around 100 tries in its figures and “beyond several hundred samples” in its abstract.

Where a program can check, tries become results. SWE-bench Lite: DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct solves 15.9% of issues with one try and 56% with 250, against a 43% single-try state of the art. The catch: that 56% is graded by the benchmark’s own tests, which an agent working on your repo may not have. For the van, the check is cheap: a program can try all 256 loads. The demo in part 3 runs it.

This is parallel search. Sequential search revises the last attempt instead; Snell et al. (Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally, §6) find easier prompts do best with revisions, harder ones with a mix.

Predict question, before anyone moves a slider: the panel asks “With a 10% false-positive verifier, as N grows without bound, which curve’s limit is 100%?” Everyone locks in. Activity: open the Best-of-N panel, move N from 1 to 1,024, then raise the false-positive rate and watch the check’s pick fall below coverage. Reveal. Debrief: the panel’s numbers are illustrative; its shape follows the paper.

Driving: sampling is cheap, selecting is not. Before asking an agent for five attempts, decide what picks the winner: tests, a checker, or you.

Transition: “A check can pick an answer. It can also train the model.”

Part 2 · Is it right? · Training

A check can also train a model to reason.

Contested

Does training sharpen what the base model could do, or expand it?

Open the sharpen-or-expand panel (illustrative)

Source: DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-R1 incentivizes reasoning in LLMs through reinforcement learning, Nature, 2025.

Speaker notes

Goal: reasoning models were trained where a rule can check the answer, so they are strongest when you hand them a check. Part 2’s answer: only a check can say whether an answer is right.

Talk track: walk the loop; this is reinforcement learning (RL). DeepSeek-R1-Zero starts from the DeepSeek-V3 base model, with no supervised fine-tuning first. For each question it writes thinking and an answer; a rule checks the final answer against ground truth, plus the format; the reward updates the weights; repeat. Nobody showed it how to reason. Its average pass@1 on AIME 2024, the share of problems solved on the first try, rose from 15.6% to 77.9% during training (Fig. 1a; 86.7% with self-consistency decoding), and its answers grew longer, with verification, reflection and alternative approaches. One caveat from the Methods: the length limit went from 32,768 to 65,536 tokens at step 8.2k, and both the score and the length jump there.

Keep R1-Zero and R1 apart: R1-Zero is the pure-RL experiment, hard to read and mixing languages; DeepSeek-R1 wraps the RL in a multistage pipeline with rejection sampling and supervised fine-tuning. The method’s name, RL with verifiable rewards, comes from Lambert et al. (Tülu 3); Act I handed it over.

The open question, labelled contested: Yue et al. (Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity…?) find RL models win at pass@1 but base models score higher pass@k at large k: RL sharpens. ProRL (Liu et al.) finds prolonged RL with KL control and resets ahead across pass@k, even where the base fails at any number of attempts: RL expands. Different models, tasks and training lengths; still open as of 2026-10-18. Do not pick a winner. If time allows, show the panel’s k slider; its curves are invented to show the crossing.

Driving: these models were trained on maths, code and tests, where answers can be checked. Give them a check: a test, an oracle, a solver.

Transition: “So: only a check can say. Next question: how much should the model think? That is a dial you set.”

Part 3 · How much thinking? · Demo

High effort: a better answer, or just a bigger bill?

Demo

Runbook: uv run effort.py live --cli claude

Then a program tries all 256 loads: the check.

The check’s answer

B, D, E and G: 62 kg, 229 EUR. 228 fills the van exactly, and is worse.

Speaker notes

Goal: the effort dial sets how much the model writes before it answers. More effort always costs; whether it helps depends on the problem, and only the check can tell.

Talk track: every vendor sells this dial. Names and levels as of 2026-10-18:

  • Anthropic: effort, --effort in Claude Code; low, medium, high, xhigh, max; Opus 5.5 defaults to medium. Anthropic calls it “a behavioral signal, not a strict token budget”.
  • OpenAI Codex: model_reasoning_effort; levels per model and client, such as low, medium, high, xhigh, max.
  • Google Gemini API: thinking_level; minimal, low, medium, high, by model. Not in the demo.

Predict question: everyone picks one chip per row on paper: how many times the thinking tokens, how many times the bill, and is each answer right this time? Then run from cursus/demos/effort: uv run effort.py live --cli claude, or uv run effort.py live --cli codex with gpt-6-astra. No tools, an empty folder. Read one row aloud and compare the ratios with the bets.

The check: open “The check’s answer” on the slide. The best load is B, D, E and G, 62 kg for 229 EUR. Not because the model says so: the recorded low-effort answer even says it “was checked by hand rather than by trying every possible subset.” The demo’s oracle tries all 256 loads: the check of part 2, outside the model. Note 228 (A, B, D, G) fills the van exactly and is still worse.

Fallback: if the network, quota or login fails, or a high run hangs, press Ctrl-C and replay the recorded run offline: uv run --offline effort.py replay. The recorded run, 2026-10-11, Claude Code 2.1.296 and codex-cli 0.162.1, all four answers 229 and correct:

  • claude-opus-5-5, low: 783 output tokens, 286 of them thinking, 9.9 s, $0.0370.
  • claude-opus-5-5, high: 964 output tokens, 447 thinking, 11.2 s, $0.0406.
  • gpt-6-astra, low: 467 output tokens, 286 reasoning, 18.6 s, $0.0562.
  • gpt-6-astra, high: 705 output tokens, 516 reasoning, 30.5 s, $0.0681.

So thinking grew about 1.6 times (Claude) and 1.8 times (Codex), the bill only 1.1 and 1.2 times: reading the prompt and the harness’s instructions costs the same at both efforts, and it is most of the bill (58% of the Claude low run was writing its 2,666 input tokens to the cache). Costs are at list prices as of 2026-10-11, rechecked 2026-10-18 and unchanged.

Debrief: if both efforts are right, say so: on this puzzle high effort bought thinking and cost, not correctness. One run is an anecdote, not a rate; a claim about effort needs many runs and a check.

Driving: the default is a choice someone made for you. Set effort per task: low for lookups and subagents, high when the problem is hard and checkable.

Transition: “Here more thinking only cost money. Sometimes it costs accuracy.”

Part 3 · How much thinking? · Lab

More thinking can cost accuracy, not just money.

Lab

Open the effort-dial panel

Turn on the inverse-scaling task. Illustrative.

Source: Aryo Pradipta Gema et al., Inverse Scaling in Test-Time Compute, arXiv, 2025.

Speaker notes

Goal: more thinking is not always better. On some problems it lowers accuracy, and it always costs. Part 3’s answer: effort is a dial you set per task, and more can hurt.

Talk track: in the demo, more thinking moved the cost, not the answer. Here it moves accuracy the wrong way. Gema et al. built tasks to look for this: counting with distractors, regression with spurious features, deduction with constraint tracking. In the fruit question (Fig. 1) the answer is always 2; with longer reasoning, models get pulled into the probability. On that task (Misleading Math, Fig. 3), DeepSeek R1 falls from 70% to 30% with five distractors, and Claude Opus 4 from nearly 100% to around 85–90% as reasoning extends. The paper names failure modes by family: Claude models grow more distracted by irrelevant information, OpenAI o-series models overfit to the framing, and all struggle to stay focused on long deductions.

One example from the appendix (I.1 in the paper’s second version): Claude Opus 4, asked how many games you have, answers 2 with no reasoning and 26 with a 16,384-token reasoning budget. These tasks were built to find the effect: a warning, not an average.

The cost side is overthinking: Chen et al. (Do NOT Think That Much for 2+3=?) asked “what is the answer of 2 plus 3?”. QwQ-32B-Preview wrote 901 tokens and 13 solutions; its first, 39 tokens in, was already right. GPT-4o answered in 7 tokens.

Activity, optional: the effort-dial panel with the inverse-scaling task on; the curve bends down past its peak. Its curves are illustrative.

Driving: if a high-effort run wanders, rerun lower, strip irrelevant context, and judge by the check, not by the length of the thinking.

Transition: “If the thinking can wander, can we at least read it to see what drove the answer?”

Part 4 · Trust what it wrote?

Most traces omit the hint that changed the answer.

Contested

Contrived multiple-choice tests on two models; harder tasks may put more of the reasoning in the trace.

Source: Anthropic, Reasoning models don’t always say what they think, anthropic.com, 2025.

Speaker notes

Goal: the written reasoning is not a faithful record of what drove the answer. Read it as a lead; check what the agent did.

Predict question: “Guess first: when the hint changed the answer, how often does the trace mention it?” Then the talk track: a trace is faithful if it reflects what actually drove the answer. Walk the three steps. Slip a hint to the answer into the question (a professor’s opinion, fake metadata, an “unauthorized access” note). Keep the cases where the answer follows the hint: then we know the hint mattered. Count how often the chain of thought says so: 25% of the time for Claude 3.7 Sonnet, 39% for DeepSeek R1, averaged over hint types. Trained with RL in environments with planted reward hacks, Claude 3.7 Sonnet exploited them on over 99% of prompts and said so under 2% of the time in most environments.

Read the limits aloud: multiple choice, contrived hints, tasks easy enough that the chain of thought was not needed. The conclusion is “check the evidence”, not “traces are useless”: a trace still tells you where to look.

In an agent, hints arrive through tool output: files, logs, web pages, test results. Measured: instructions planted in retrieved data steer LLM applications (indirect prompt injection, Greshake et al., Not what you’ve signed up for). Not measured: how often a cue in a tool result goes unmentioned in the trace; that is an extrapolation. Hypothetical illustration: a README says the tests in legacy/ are flaky, skip them; the agent skips them and reports “all relevant tests pass”, without naming the README. The transcript of tool calls is better evidence than the reasoning text. Act V returns to injection.

Driving: read the agent’s reasoning as a lead, not as evidence. Check what it did: the diff, the commands, the test run.

Transition: “Even with no hint at all, the explanation can be about a different process.”

Part 4 · Trust what it wrote?

The model computes one way and explains another.

Asked how it did it

“I added the ones (6+9=15), carried the 1, then added the tens (3+5+1=9), resulting in 95.”

Source: Jack Lindsey et al., On the Biology of a Large Language Model, transformer-circuits.pub, 2025.

Speaker notes

Goal: a model’s account of how it got a result is a story about the result, not a record of the computation. Part 4’s answer: what it wrote is a lead, not evidence.

Predict question: “How would you add 36 + 59 in your head?” Most say: carry the 1. Talk track: attribution graphs trace which internal features drive an output (§Addition). For 36 + 59, one pathway estimates the size of the sum at low precision, “the sum is near 92”, another gets the last digit exactly, and the two combine into 95. Then they asked the model how it did it. It describes the school carry algorithm, the way people are taught, which the traced computation did not use. The answer is right; the explanation is about a different process. The authors: a capability without “metacognitive” insight into it. One model, one prompt: a vivid case, not a rate.

Back to the van: the visible answer in part 1 states 229 first, then justifies it. Those steps are an explanation, not necessarily the route; the route, if there was one, was in the 286 hidden thinking tokens.

Driving: when it matters, ask for something you can rerun: the test, the computation, the command.

Transition: “Last question: what does the model know about the world it reasons about?”

Part 5 · What does it know?

“World model” means three things. Keep them apart.

The van’s whole world was in the prompt. Your repo’s is not.

  1. Meaning 1 Inside the network Othello-GPT, shown only moves, holds the board inside.
  2. Meaning 2 A simulator Predicts what comes next, to plan.
  3. Meaning 3 Your repo The agent’s picture, from what it read and ran.

Source: Kenneth Li et al., Emergent World Representations, arXiv, 2022.

Speaker notes

Goal: “world model” is used for three different things; evidence for one is not evidence for the others. The one students control is the third.

Talk track: for the van, the whole world was in the prompt: eight parcels, 63 kg. Most problems are not like that. A model reasons from what it learned, and an agent from what it has read about your repo. That is what people mean by a world model, in three senses.

Meaning 1, a representation inside the network. Othello-GPT is trained only to predict legal Othello moves, with no rules given. Probes read a nonlinear representation of the board out of its activations, and editing that internal board changes the moves it predicts: the intervention is what makes it more than a correlation. A second case, planning: Claude 3.5 Haiku, writing a couplet after “…grab it”, already has features for “rabbit” and “habit” active at the line break; suppress “rabbit” and the line ends in “habit” (Lindsey et al., On the Biology of a Large Language Model, §Planning in Poems).

Meaning 2, a learned simulator: predict what happens next given an action, then plan through it. LeCun’s Mode-2 from part 1 plans through exactly this kind of model. Background, if asked; makers’ claims, not independent evaluations: Meta’s V-JEPA 2 (2025-06-11) plans robot pick-and-place toward image goals; Google DeepMind’s Genie 3 (2025-08-05) turns a text prompt into an interactive world at 24 fps and 720p; NVIDIA’s Cosmos 3 (2026-05-31) is an open world foundation model; AMI Labs, LeCun’s company, launched on 2026-03-10 with a $1.03B round and states this as its aim (launch, home page). None of them runs inside your coding agent.

Meaning 3 is the one you control; it is the last slide of the act.

Driving: when someone says a model “has a world model”, ask which meaning, and what the evidence is.

Transition: “Tracking state is one thing. Having the law that generates it is another.”

Part 5 · What does it know? · Lab

Kepler without Newton: good predictions, the wrong laws.

Lab

Open the Kepler panel

The fit matches the orbits until you add a mass. Illustrative.

Source: Keyon Vafa et al., What Has a Foundation Model Found?, arXiv, 2025.

Speaker notes

Goal: predicting well is not knowing the law. A model can fit what it has seen and still be wrong where the world differs.

Talk track: Kepler’s laws predicted the orbits; Newton’s explained them and carried over to new problems. Vafa et al. train a transformer on orbital trajectories: it predicts them well. Then they fine-tune it to predict forces, and run symbolic regression on its force predictions. The law it recovers is nonsense: for one galaxy sample, the formula on the slide; for each of five samples, a different formula, never the true law. The control: the same procedure on a model given the true state recovers Newton. Their reading: the model behaves as if it learned task-specific heuristics that do not generalize. Fitting the data is not having the model that generates it.

Activity: open the Kepler panel of the world-model lab. Let the fit match the orbits, then move the slider that adds a mass and watch the prediction break while the true orbit changes. The panel is a stylized simulation, not the paper’s data.

Driving: an agent that has seen many repos like yours can predict well and still be wrong where yours differs. Watch for confident pattern-matching after something changed.

Transition: “So what keeps the agent’s picture of your repo right?”

Part 5 · What does it know? · Meaning 3 · Lab

Your repo, as the agent pictures it: feed it observations.

Lab

Open the planner panel

Plan once, or replan. Illustrative.

Speaker notes

Goal: the agent’s picture of your repo comes from what it read and ran, and it goes stale. Frequent observations, fast tests and clear errors, keep it right. Part 5’s answer: a picture to keep fed.

Talk track: meaning 3 is the one you control. The agent’s picture of your repo comes from what it read: files, AGENTS.md, earlier output. Test output is the observation that corrects it. In LeCun’s terms (A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence, §3 and §3.1), the world model is adjusted once the next observation is available, and Mode-2 replans from it, as model-predictive control does.

Activity: open the planner panel of the world-model lab and set it exactly like this; these are also the lab’s defaults:

At these settings the open loop hits the obstacle, and the closed loop replans and reaches the goal: the two rows on the slide. Map it: the dynamics model is the picture of the repo, the observation is test output, replanning is the agent loop. Say the limit out loud: this is an illustrative run where feedback corrects a moderate error in the picture; with a much worse model the closed loop can fail too, as the lab’s closing card says.

Think-pair-share: “On your own task, what could check the agent’s answer, and how often does the agent see that check?” Listen for pairs with no check to name.

Driving: make observations cheap and frequent: a fast test command, clear errors, an AGENTS.md that says how to run things. Correct the agent’s picture early.

Transition: “Five questions, five answers.”

Close · Five answers, five habits

Writing plus a check does what one glance cannot.

  1. 1 · It writes. Ask for the steps before the answer.
  2. 2 · A check. Give it a test, an oracle, a solver.
  3. 3 · A dial. Set effort per task.
  4. 4 · A lead. Read the diff, not the story.
  5. 5 · A picture. Feed it: fast tests, clear errors.
Speaker notes

Goal: students leave with the act’s five answers, now all filled in on the strip, and the habit each one gives them.

Talk track: one forward pass is a glance. Writing lets a model chain steps, and a check outside the model turns many tries, or a training run, into results. Read the strip, then the habit under each answer:

  • It writes: for multi-step work, let the agent write a plan or steps before it acts.
  • Only a check can say: give the agent a test, an oracle or a solver, and trust that, not its say-so.
  • A dial; more can hurt: set effort per task, low for lookups, high for hard and checkable problems.
  • A lead, not evidence: read the reasoning to know where to look, then check the diff, the commands, the test run.
  • A picture to keep fed: fast tests, clear errors and an AGENTS.md keep the agent’s picture of your repo right.

Both labs stay open after class.
