Tokens, not letters

A language model never sees the letters you type. A tokenizer first cuts the text into pieces and replaces each piece with an integer ID; the model reads only those integers.

Predict first

Answer both questions and lock in before you look at the playground below: it gives the answer away.

In GPT-2 (r50k_base, the default encoding below), how many tokens is “ strawberry”, with its leading space?

One token in both encodings. In GPT-2’s r50k_base, “ strawberry” is the single ID 41236; in o200k_base it is the single ID 101830. Both decode back to the whole piece “ strawberry”.

The leading space belongs to the token. Without it, “strawberry” splits into three pieces, “st”, “raw”, “berry”, in both encodings.

How many r's in “ strawberry” can the model see?

The text has three r's, but the model receives one integer, 41236 in r50k_base (or 101830 in o200k_base), not the letters s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y.

It can learn letter facts only indirectly, from training text that happens to spell out what an ID contains. That is why counting letters is unreliable, and why the dependable move is to have the model write code that counts them.

A character can take more than one token: “é” or “🍓” may be split into bytes. Such a character gets one chip with a ×2 or ×3 badge, and hovering it highlights every ID it took.

Why not Claude’s tokenizer?

Claude’s tokenizer is not public (as of 2026-10-13), so this page uses OpenAI’s encodings: GPT-2’s r50k_base and GPT-4o’s o200k_base. These IDs are not Claude’s, and Claude’s counts differ. Anthropic offers an online token counting endpoint: it counts tokens for a message, but it does not publish a tokenizer or its IDs.
