Where agents are now · Mathematics

How an OpenAI model proved a conjecture open since 1991.

  1. 1 · Pose. One of about 4,000 open problems given to the same model.
  2. 2 · Prove. About 3 hours of thinking per result, on average; some build on earlier ones.
  3. 3 · Check. A program, not a person, verifies every step.
  4. 4 · Release. Published as raw data; people judge what it means.
  5. You don’t need to follow the math. By session 5, you will run research this way.

Source: OpenAI, Local smoothing for the Euclidean wave equation, 2026, and its release notes in github.com/openai/math.

Speaker notes

Talk track: we start at the frontier. In 1991 Christopher Sogge asked how much a wave can grow when it comes to a focus, averaged over a short time. Several famous problems follow from it, among them Kakeya: a set holding a needle in every direction, however thin, has full dimension. Press each step as you say it.

1, Pose. People settled two dimensions in 2019. OpenAI gave the question to an unreleased model, one of about 4,000 open problems, most posed the same way.

2, Prove. About three hours of thinking per result, on average. In September the model settled three dimensions and part of four, in four papers; on 18 October, every dimension, building on those papers and on people’s.

3, Check. The next day Lean, a program that checks every step of a proof, accepted it. It checks the proof, not that the statement is the right one: people still read that.

4, Release. Published on 26 October as raw data. Experts are reading the 59-page paper (no verdict yet on 7 November 2026). In an earlier batch, three papers were withdrawn after a sign error.

5. You don’t need any of this math. Watch the steps: pose, prove, check, release. By session 5, you run research that way.

The cursus

Each session hands an agent a bigger piece of your research.

Speaker notes

Here is what this technology opens for a scientist: one new thing per session.

Today, Fundamentals: what a model and an agent really are, then you drive one, up to a word problem turned into a model a checker certifies.

Research agents: a team of agents drafts a literature review, and a write-up from your own experiment logs, with every citation traceable.

Remote agents: the same agent runs headless in a cloud sandbox; you file an issue and get back a pull request with its evidence, laptop closed.

Deploying: every merge re-solves the canonical instance on a pinned open solver and regenerates the study.

The Arena: a cohort tournament on a rolling-horizon problem; you improve your policy by filing issues to agents, and it is ranked on instances nobody has seen.

One problem family runs through all five. Tonight’s take-home is an electric-truck middle-mile network, the world I work in.

Together they cover the steps of the OpenAI run you just saw: pose a problem, let agents work on it, check the result, release it so others can check it too.

Fundamentals · Today

What you will learn today.

Speaker notes

Talk track: here is today, in four acts; point at each box as you name it.

Act I, the sampler: how a large language model writes: one pass through the network, then one draw, one token at a time. Everything else today builds on it.

Act II, thinking: how it reasons by writing out steps, how much thinking to ask for, and when to trust its answer: only a check can say it is right.

Act III, the agent: the model only writes text. Something else runs the tools it asks for and hands it the results, in a loop, toward a goal; that, plus the model, is an agent.

Act IV, your turn: you drive an agent on one small problem, from reading it to turning it in. The homework repeats those steps on a bigger problem.

The OpenAI run you saw had the same pieces at a far larger scale: a model that writes and thinks, and a check at the end.
