How it is graded

The rule: feasibility proven by the checker counts more than the objective value.

For P01, grading reruns the checker and the data from main on the solution.json you turned in, then reads your p01_model.py. It asks three questions, in this order:

  1. Does the checker prove the plan feasible? Coverage, sequence, maintenance, charging and SoC must all pass.
  2. Did your model produce that plan? The plan must come from your model, not from a hand-edited file.
  3. Only then: how far is the objective from the known optimum?

A correct model stopped at a feasible incumbent beats a wrong model with a lower number. A plan that claims a lower cost but fails a feasibility contract is not a better answer. It is not an answer at all.

Parts 1 and 2 are read against their “What a good answer looks like” blocks below. Only what is on your homework branch is graded. Copies of the checker, the data or the contract suite on your branch are not trusted.

C · Cold open: a word problem to a certified LP

Run it twice, and start each run clean. In a terminal, go to sessions/01-fundamentals/cursus/demos/cold-open/ and start a fresh session there with claude or codex. Open prompt.txt, then paste its whole text as your first message. Approve the agent’s uv run python work/model.py when it asks. Before you exit, copy the seven printed lines into your notes, and the agent’s work/model.py as well. Then clear the run from that folder with:

git clean -fdx -- .

This deletes the agent’s work/ folder and nothing that git tracks.

In class (recorded 2026-10-10, rehearsal): two runs with Claude Code 2.1.296 (claude-opus-5-5) and two with Codex 0.160.0 (gpt-6-astra). Every run printed the same certificate: status: Optimal, tables=20, chairs=20, duals 20, 10 and 0, primal and dual objective 1400, a duality gap of about 2.3e-13 and binding constraints assembly and carpentry. The programs differed. Claude Code checked HiGHS’s dual signs before trusting them. Codex flipped them on a condition in one run and took abs() in the other.

What a good answer looks like

  • It names the two demos, your agent and its version, and the model that answered.
  • It writes down the class result and your own runs as they came out: the names, the counts, or the seven certificate lines. It does not summarize them.
  • It says what changed, both from the class and between your runs, and what stayed the same. For example: “3 of 5 distinct, against 5 of 5 in class”, “the count ranged from 121 to 150”, or “a different program, the same certificate”.
  • It gives a reason for each change. Each answer is a fresh draw from a distribution. A different model or version changes that distribution. The model reads tokens, not letters. Two programs can both be checked against one certificate.

A weak answer says “the results were a bit different” without numbers or a reason.

Part 2 · Three sentences on “sampling, not retrieval”

In home-notes.md, explain in exactly three sentences of your own what “sampling, not retrieval” means for an LLM, and why it matters when you work with an agent. Write them yourself, not with your agent: the point is to check your own understanding. Use your part 1 runs as evidence. The next-token lab lets you see the distribution again.

What a good answer looks like

  • Mechanism. One forward pass gives a probability distribution over the next token, and the reply is drawn from it one token at a time.
  • Not a lookup. No stored document or database row is fetched. A fluent, confident reply is generated, so being plausible is not being true, and a citation or number can be invented.
  • Consequence. The same prompt can give different answers, as your part 1 runs showed, so you check the output with something deterministic: run the code, or call the checker or the solver. Re-asking does not count as checking.

A weak answer says the model “searches its training data”, or repeats the phrase without saying what is drawn from what.

Part 3 · Solve P01 with your own agent

P01 is the take-home that the course comes back to: EV linehaul scheduling and charging, with 40 legs, 10 electric trucks and eight depots. The statement is in homework-p01/PROBLEM.md, and the task, the solution format and how to read the checker are in homework-p01/README.md. Your agent writes the model. Your job is to set the contract and to believe only what the checker certifies.

  1. Read PROBLEM.md yourself first.
  2. In your agent, started in sessions/01-fundamentals/, give it this prompt. It is the same for both agents. 
    Read exercises/homework-p01/README.md and exercises/homework-p01/PROBLEM.md. Build exercises/homework-p01/p01_model.py as a MILP on HiGHS through highspy, keeping the API of the stub. Do not edit exercises/homework-p01/data/, p01_checker.py or p01_contract.py. Run the model, then the checker and the contract suite with the commands in the README, and fix the model until the checker prints PASS. Show me the checker output.
  3. Run the proof yourself, and do not take your agent’s word for it: 
    uv run python exercises/homework-p01/p01_model.py
uv run python exercises/homework-p01/p01_checker.py
uv run pytest exercises/homework-p01/p01_contract.py
  4. When a check fails, give the failure line to your agent. Each line starts with the contract that broke. Write the verdict and the failures you met into home-notes.md.

What a good answer looks like

  • Best: solution.json has status optimal, the checker’s first line is PASS, and its summary gives the recomputed objective with its breakdown, the known optimum and a gap of 0.
  • Still a good answer: a time limit stopped the solve at a feasible incumbent (status feasible in solution.json), and every feasibility contract passes. Only optimality fails, and your notes give the gap. Under the grading rule, this beats any infeasible plan, whatever its cost.
  • p01_model.py is your agent’s model with the stub’s API, and running it produces the solution.json you turn in. You have not edited the file by hand.
  • You ran the contract suite, and data/, p01_checker.py and p01_contract.py are unchanged.
  • Your notes give the checker verdict and one or two contracts that failed on the way, with what the agent changed to fix them.

A weak answer reports a low objective that the checker rejects, or a plan the model does not produce.

Part 4 · Turn it in

One command, the same for every session this term. In your agent:

Claude Code

/turn-in 01

Codex

$turn-in 01

Without an agent, run it from the repository root in a terminal (Git Bash on Windows):

bash scripts/turn-in.sh 01

The turn-in first runs exercises/turn-in-check.sh, which runs the P01 checker on your solution.json. A malformed solution.json or an edited data/ stops it, so you can fix the problem and run it again. An infeasible or suboptimal plan is reported and still turned in. It then commits only sessions/01-fundamentals/exercises/ to your own branch, homework/s01/<handle>, where <handle> is your GitHub login, and pushes it. It never pushes to main. If you have no push access to the course repo, it pushes to your fork and also prints a compare URL.

You can run it as often as you like. Every run adds a commit, and the history keeps every submission. Homework branches are public, because the course repo is public, so keep secrets and personal data out of your notes. If the turn-in refuses, read its message and fix the cause. Never work around it with --force or --no-verify.

What a good answer looks like

  • The run ends with your branch URL, https://github.com/<course or your fork>/tree/homework/s01/<handle>. From a fork, it also prints a line starting Compare with the course:.
  • On that page, the latest commit is Turn in session 01 homework (<handle>). It holds exercises/home-notes.md, exercises/homework-p01/p01_model.py and exercises/homework-p01/solution.json.
  • The checker verdict printed during the turn-in matches the one in your notes.
